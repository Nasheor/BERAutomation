# BER Automation — Country-Specific Data Reference

**Project:** CIRCUS — Connecting Initiatives for Rural Communities, Upscaling their Sustainable Energy
**Programme:** Interreg North-West Europe (NWE)
**Document scope:** All country-specific parameters used in the HWB calculation engine,
their sources, and the effect they have on results.

---

## 1. Introduction & Methodology

### 1.1 The HWB Annual Balance Method

The tool uses the **HWB (Heizwärmebedarf — annual heating demand)** method, ported from an
Austrian/German building assessment Excel tool developed at MTU (Benjamin Kaiser, Jan 2025).
It follows the approach described in *"Leitfaden für die Berechnung des Heizwaermebedarfs"*
(Die Umweltberatung, 2019).

The calculation determines the building's annual energy balance from first principles:

```
Q_heating = Q_transmission + Q_ventilation − Q_internal_gains − Q_solar_gains
```

Where:
- **Q_transmission** — heat lost through the building envelope (walls, roof, floor, windows),
  determined by U-values, areas, and Heating Degree Days (HDD)
- **Q_ventilation** — heat lost through air exchange, determined by volume and HDD
- **Q_internal_gains** — heat generated by occupants and appliances (3.75 W/m² net floor area)
- **Q_solar_gains** — passive solar heat through windows, determined by orientation, glazing
  area, g-values, and solar irradiance during the heating season

Hot water demand is added separately, then final energy is obtained by dividing by heating
system efficiency (or SCOP for heat pumps). Primary energy is obtained by multiplying final
energy by a **Primary Energy Factor (PEF)**. CO₂ emissions are obtained by multiplying final
energy by a **CO₂ emission factor**.

### 1.2 Why Country-Specific Data Matters

The same physical building produces very different energy assessments depending on:

| Factor | Why it varies | Magnitude of effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Heating Degree Days** | Climate — how cold and how long the winter is | ±50% on heating demand |
| **Solar irradiance** | Latitude and climate — how much passive solar gain | ±15% on net demand |
| **Electricity CO₂ factor** | Grid mix (nuclear, wind, coal, hydro) | Up to **7×** difference |
| **Primary energy factor (PEF)** | National regulation under EPBD | ±80% on primary energy |
| **Native EPC scale** | Each country has its own certificate and thresholds | Results look very different |

Failing to account for these country-specific factors produces incorrect CO₂ estimates
(especially for heat pumps) and misleading EPC ratings.

### 2.2 Heating Degree Days (HDD, base 15.5°C)

HDD measure the cumulative cold experienced over a heating season. A day with a mean
temperature of 5.5°C contributes 10 HDD (15.5 − 5.5 = 10). Higher HDD means more
heat loss through the building envelope and more energy needed for heating.

**Source:** degreedays.net, 2022 data.

| Country | HDD (base 15.5°C) | Relative to Ireland |
|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 2,149 | — (reference) |
| France (NW) | 1,462 | −32% milder |
| Germany (NW) | 2,157 | +0.4% similar |
| Belgium | 1,826 | −15% milder |
| Netherlands | 1,921 | −11% milder |
| Luxembourg | 2,140 | −0.4% similar |
| Switzerland (Basel) | 2,415 | +12% colder |
| Austria | 3,400 | +58% colder (placeholder) |

**Implication:** A gas-heated pre-1980 building in France will need ~32% less heating energy
than the identical building in Ireland, simply because the winters are milder.

## 3. Electricity CO₂ Emission Factors

### 3.1 Why This Matters

The CO₂ factor for electricity varies dramatically by country because it depends on
the **electricity generation mix** — how much comes from nuclear, renewables, gas, coal, etc.
This only affects **electric heating systems**: electric direct, heat pump (air/ground/water).

For fossil fuel heating (oil, gas, biomass), the CO₂ factor is determined by the fuel's
carbon content and is effectively the same across all countries.

### 3.2 Country Values

**Source:** National grid operators and statistical agencies, 2022 reporting year.

| Country | CO₂ factor (g/kWh) | Dominant generation | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 210 | Gas + wind | SEAI Conversion Factors 2022 |
| France | **52** | **Nuclear (70%+)** | RTE Bilan Électrique 2022 |
| Germany | **385** | **Coal + gas** | UBA Emissionsfaktoren 2022 |
| Belgium | 163 | Nuclear + gas | ELIA grid data 2022 |
| Netherlands | 290 | Gas + wind | RVO/CBS Energie in Nederland 2022 |
| Luxembourg | 197 | Net importer (FR/DE mix) | ILR/STATEC 2022 |
| Switzerland | **29** | **Hydro + nuclear** | SFOE Elektrizitätsstatistik 2022 |
| Austria | 156 | Hydro + gas | E-Control Ökostromstatistik 2022 |

### 3.3 Impact Example: Heat Pump CO₂ per m²/year

Using the standard test building (10×8m, 2-storey, detached, pre-1980, air-source heat pump):

| Country | CO₂ kg/m²/yr | vs Ireland |
|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 8.72 | — |
| France | **1.39** | **−84%** |
| Germany | **16.43** | **+88%** |
| Belgium | 5.67 | −35% |
| Netherlands | 10.52 | +21% |

**Key insight:** Installing a heat pump in France produces ~84% less CO₂ than in Ireland
because French electricity is nearly carbon-free. In Germany, the same heat pump produces
88% *more* CO₂ than in Ireland because the grid is coal-heavy. This is a critical
consideration for CIRCUS project partners advising communities on heating upgrades.

### 3.4 Fossil Fuel CO₂ Factors (country-independent)

These are determined by fuel combustion chemistry and are uniform across all countries.

| Fuel | kg CO₂/kWh | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Oil boiler | 0.264 | SEAI / IPCC |
| Gas boiler | 0.194 | SEAI / IPCC (weighted gas/natural gas) |
| Biomass | 0.000 | Carbon-neutral by EU convention |
| District heating | 0.180 | Approximate average (varies by system) |

> **Note on district heating:** The 0.180 kg/kWh value is a reasonable average for
> district heating networks fuelled by gas/CHP. In practice, CO₂ varies widely —
> waste-heat networks (common in Netherlands and Luxembourg) can be near-zero,
> while older coal-fed networks (some German cities) can exceed 0.300 kg/kWh.
> For accurate CIRCUS project results, use the local network's published factor.

## 4. Primary Energy Factors (PEF)

### 4.1 What Is a Primary Energy Factor?

The PEF converts **final (delivered) energy** — the energy you purchase and use —
into **primary energy** — the total energy extracted from nature, including generation
and transmission losses. For a gas boiler, PEF ≈ 1.1 (10% upstream losses). For
electricity from a gas power station at 40% efficiency, PEF ≈ 2.5.

Primary energy is the basis for all official EPC ratings across Europe because it
captures the full energy chain, not just what enters the building.

### 4.2 Why PEF for Electricity Varies by Country

Each EU member state sets its own PEF for electricity in their national EPBD
(Energy Performance of Buildings Directive) transposition. The value reflects
the national grid mix and long-term policy intent. Countries with cleaner grids
(France, Netherlands) have lower effective waste ratios, but regulatory values
can differ from the actual grid emissions factor.

**Source:** National building regulations documents.

| Country | Electricity PEF | Regulation | Non-electric fuels |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 2.08 | SEAI / S.I. 259/2016 | 1.10 |
| France | 2.58 | RE2020 (Réglementation Environnementale) | 1.00 |
| Germany | 1.80 | GEG 2020 (Gebäudeenergiegesetz) | 1.10 |
| Belgium | 2.50 | EPB Regulations (Flanders/Wallonia) | 1.00 |
| Netherlands | **1.45** | NTA 8800:2022 | 1.00 |
| Luxembourg | 2.00 | Règlement Grand-Ducal 2016 | 1.00 |
| Switzerland | 2.00 | SIA 380/1:2016 | 1.00 |
| Austria | 2.60 | OIB Richtlinie 6:2019 | 1.10 |

### 4.3 Impact Example: Heat Pump Primary Energy

The PEF directly scales the primary energy output — the number used for the EPC rating.
Using the same standard building with an air-source heat pump (SCOP 3.5):

| Country | Final energy kWh/m²/yr | PEF | Primary energy kWh/m²/yr | BER band |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 41.5 | 2.08 | 86.3 | B1 |
| France | 26.8 | 2.58 | 69.2 | A3 |
| Germany | 42.7 | 1.80 | 76.8 | B1 |
| Belgium | 34.7 | 2.50 | 86.9 | B1 |
| Netherlands | 36.3 | 1.45 | **52.6** | **A3** |

The Netherlands gets the best primary energy result because its PEF (1.45) is the
lowest in NWE, reflecting its EPBD policy position. France gets a good result because
of its mild climate (lower final energy) despite having a high PEF (2.58).

> **Important:** The PEF does NOT affect CO₂ calculations. CO₂ is computed directly
> from final energy × country CO₂ factor. PEF only affects the kWh/m²/yr primary
> energy figure used for EPC band assignment.

## 5. Native EPC Rating Scales

### 5.1 Overview

Every EU country has its own Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) system. While all
are based on the EPBD framework and use primary energy kWh/m²/yr as the core metric,
each has different band names, threshold values, and number of classes. The tool
computes **both** the Irish BER (as a common cross-border reference) and the native
country EPC simultaneously.

## 6. CIRCUS Partner Country Profiles

The CIRCUS project (NWE0200304) operates in **five countries**: Ireland, France, Germany,
Belgium, and the Netherlands, with partners including Munster Technological University (IE),
South Kerry Development Partnership (IE), Énergies citoyennes en Pays de Vilaine (FR),
Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (DE), Landelijke Gilden (BE), LochemEnergie (NL), and
University of Twente (NL).

### 6.1 Ireland

- **Climate:** Cool oceanic. Mild winters (HDD 2,149) but with very low solar irradiance.
  Heating season ~219 days/yr.
- **Building stock:** Dominated by detached and semi-detached houses, heavily reliant on
  oil boilers (especially rural areas) and gas boilers (urban). Older pre-1980 stock
  is typically solid wall with single glazing.
- **Electricity grid:** ~210 g/kWh CO₂ (gas + wind). Declining rapidly as wind capacity grows.
- **EPC system:** BER (Building Energy Rating), SEAI. Scale A1–G.
- **Key challenge:** High reliance on oil heating in rural communities — prime target for
  heat pump retrofits and community energy.

### 6.2 France

- **Climate:** Mild, particularly in north-west (Le Mans reference station). Shorter heating
  season (187 days) and lower HDD (1,462). Mild winters mean lower base heating demand.
- **Building stock:** Mix of stone farmhouses (granites, tufa, sandstone), rendered blockwork,
  and modern timber-frame. Post-2010 builds follow RT2012/RE2020.
- **Electricity grid:** ~52 g/kWh CO₂ — one of the cleanest in Europe due to nuclear dominance
  (~70–75% of generation). Heat pumps have exceptional CO₂ performance in France.
- **EPC system:** DPE (Diagnostic de Performance Énergétique), reformed July 2021. Scale A–G.
  Also includes a separate CO₂ class.
- **Key challenge:** Many rural stone buildings are very hard and expensive to insulate from
  the outside; interior insulation is often used but reduces floor area.

### 6.3 Germany

- **Climate:** Continental, particularly in NW region (Paderborn). HDD 2,157, similar to
  Ireland. Cloudier winters means lower south solar irradiance (331 kWh/m²) during heating.
- **Building stock:** Strong tradition of brick/block construction. Significant older stock
  with cavity or solid-wall construction. Post-2009 buildings follow EnEV energy regulations,
  post-2020 follow GEG.
- **Electricity grid:** ~385 g/kWh CO₂ — still among the highest in NWE due to coal and gas.
  Heat pumps give poorer CO₂ performance than in France/Switzerland. Grid is decarbonising
  with renewable expansion.
- **EPC system:** Energieausweis (GEG 2020), scale A+–H. Stricter thresholds than Irish BER —
  a German H-rated building would be BER G in Ireland.
- **Key challenge:** Many rural buildings have been insulated with external render systems but
  still have poor window performance and old gas/oil boilers.

### 6.4 Belgium

- **Climate:** Temperate oceanic, similar to Netherlands. HDD 1,826 (milder than Ireland).
  Heating season ~209 days.
- **Building stock:** Brick row houses (terraced) are very common in Flanders and Wallonia.
  Many pre-1980 buildings with cavity walls but no fill insulation.
- **Electricity grid:** ~163 g/kWh CO₂. Mix of nuclear (~40%, being phased out), gas, and wind.
- **EPC system:** Belgium has THREE regional EPC systems (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels). The
  tool uses Flanders (EPB) as reference. All three use primary energy but with different
  thresholds and mandatory inspection requirements.
- **Key challenge:** Belgium has strong regional variation — Flanders has more aggressive
  energy renovation policy than Wallonia. Rural community energy projects span both regions.

### 6.5 Netherlands

- **Climate:** Maritime, flat topography creates good wind exposure. HDD 1,921. High solar
  irradiance during heating season for north-facing orientations (162 kWh/m²).
- **Building stock:** Compact terraced brick houses are dominant in towns; rural areas have
  more detached farmhouses. Netherlands has very strong insulation standards post-2010.
- **Electricity grid:** ~290 g/kWh CO₂. Natural gas dominant but large offshore wind is
  decarbonising the grid rapidly.
- **EPC system:** Energielabel (NTA 8800:2022), scale A++++–G. The Netherlands' new NTA 8800
  method uses a low PEF for electricity (1.45) — **the lowest in NWE** — giving heat pump
  buildings significantly better primary energy ratings than in Ireland or France.
- **Key challenge:** The Netherlands is one of the most gas-dependent countries in Europe
  for heating; government policy is pushing hard on heat pump adoption and district heating.

## 7. Worked Examples

### 7.1 Standard Test Building

All examples use:
- **Dimensions:** 10m × 8m footprint, 2 storeys, 3m storey height
- **Type:** Detached
- **Construction:** Pre-1980 (solid walls, single glazing — worst-case existing stock)

### 7.2 Gas Boiler Scenario

A pre-1980 building with a standard gas boiler in each CIRCUS partner country:

| Country | Primary energy kWh/m²/yr | CO₂ kg/m²/yr | Irish BER | Native EPC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 177.6 | 31.32 | C2 | C2 (BER) |
| France | 114.7 | 20.22 | B2 | C (DPE) |
| Germany | 182.6 | 32.20 | C2 | F (Energieausweis) |
| Belgium | 148.7 | 26.23 | B3 | B (EPC Flanders) |
| Netherlands | 155.2 | 27.37 | C1 | A (Energielabel) |

**Reading these results:**
- The same building in Germany shows **F** on the Energieausweis — German regulations
  are strict, and a building at 182 kWh/m²/yr is classified as very poor.
- The same building in the Netherlands shows **A** on the Energielabel — because the Dutch
  PEF for gas (1.0 in NTA 8800) and the scale thresholds make A quite achievable even for
  an inefficient gas-heated building. This highlights why comparing across countries without
  using native scales leads to misinterpretation.
- CO₂ figures are broadly similar across countries for gas boilers (because gas combustion
  CO₂ is the same everywhere), with France slightly lower due to its milder climate
  reducing the total gas burned.

### 7.3 Air-Source Heat Pump Scenario

The same building retrofitted with an air-source heat pump (SCOP 3.5):

| Country | Primary energy kWh/m²/yr | CO₂ kg/m²/yr | Irish BER | Native EPC | CO₂ saving vs gas |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 86.3 | 8.72 | B1 | B1 (BER) | −72% |
| France | 69.2 | **1.39** | A3 | **A (DPE)** | −93% |
| Germany | 76.8 | **16.43** | B1 | C (Energieausweis) | −49% |
| Belgium | 86.9 | 5.67 | B1 | A (EPC Flanders) | −78% |
| Netherlands | **52.6** | 10.52 | **A3** | **A+ (Energielabel)** | −62% |

**Reading these results:**
- **France** has by far the best CO₂ outcome from a heat pump: 1.39 kg/m²/yr — a 93%
  reduction vs gas. This is almost entirely due to France's nuclear-dominated electricity grid.
- **Germany** gets the worst CO₂ result from a heat pump: 16.43 kg/m²/yr — actually *worse*
  than the gas boiler in Ireland (31.32). Heat pumps in Germany only save CO₂ compared to
  their own gas boilers because German gas boilers emit more (32.20 kg/m²/yr vs German heat
  pump 16.43). As the German grid decarbonises, this improves rapidly.
- **Netherlands** gets the best primary energy result (52.6 kWh/m²/yr) because of its very
  low electricity PEF (1.45), giving an A3 BER and A+ Energielabel.
- **Primary energy vs CO₂ can point in opposite directions:** In Germany, the heat pump has
  lower primary energy (76.8) than gas (182.6) — because the PEF for electricity (1.80) is
  far lower than the savings from SCOP 3.5. But the CO₂ is higher than in Belgium because
  the German grid burns more fossil fuel per kWh.

### 7.4 Retrofit Impact Example (Ireland, pre-1980 detached)

Starting point: 10×8m, pre-1980, gas boiler, Ireland → BER C2 (177.6 kWh/m²/yr)

| Retrofit measure | Primary energy kWh/m²/yr | BER band | CO₂ kg/m²/yr | Saving |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No retrofit | 177.6 | C2 | 31.32 | — |
| +12cm wall / +20cm roof / triple glazing | ~130 | B3 | ~22.9 | −27% |
| Above + switch to heat pump | ~50 | A2 | ~4.3 | −86% |

The most impactful single measure is switching from gas to a heat pump (large CO₂ reduction),
but the building must first be well-insulated to avoid oversizing the heat pump and to ensure
thermal comfort.

## 8. Limitations and Known Approximations

### 8.1 U-Values Are Austrian-Sourced

The default U-values for each construction epoch are sourced from Austrian OIB guidelines
(via the original Excel tool). While these are representative of central European building
practice, there are national differences:

- Pre-1980 Irish buildings often used 9-inch solid concrete block walls (U ≈ 2.0–2.5 W/m²K),
  potentially worse than the Austrian reference (U_wall = 1.20).
- French stone buildings can have very thick walls (U ≈ 1.0–1.5) that provide thermal mass
  but poor steady-state insulation.
- German Fachwerk (half-timbered) buildings have very poor wall performance.

For detailed analysis, use manual window/wall U-value inputs to override defaults.

### 8.2 CO₂ Factors Are Static (2022)

Electricity grid carbon intensity changes year to year as renewable capacity is added.
Germany's grid was ~385 g/kWh in 2022 but is declining by approximately 20–30 g/kWh
per year as wind and solar expand. Ireland's grid (currently ~210 g/kWh) will likely
fall below 100 g/kWh by 2030 as offshore wind develops. The tool uses 2022 values
as a stable reference point.

### 8.3 Belgian Regional EPC Variation

The tool uses Flanders (EPB) thresholds for Belgium. Wallonia uses a slightly different
scale and Brussels has its own system. For Brussels or Wallonia projects, the native EPC
band displayed may not match the officially issued certificate.

### 8.4 Swiss GEAK Approximation

The official GEAK uses two separate ratings: *thermal energy demand* (heating only) and
*overall energy demand* (all energy uses). The tool's single primary energy metric is
an approximation. For official GEAK certification, the Swiss SIA 380/1 calculation
must be performed with canton-specific tools.

### 8.5 District Heating CO₂

The district heating CO₂ factor (0.180 kg/kWh) is a European average approximation.
Actual values vary enormously by network. Partners in Netherlands and Luxembourg should
check their local network's published factor (often available from the network operator)
and use the `overrides` parameter or manual input to specify it.

### 8.6 HWB Volume Formula

The volume calculation in the Excel tool (P2 formula) multiplies net floor area by both
storey height and storey count, which can appear to double-count storeys. The tool
faithfully replicates the Excel formula for consistency with the source methodology.
This does not affect the specific energy result (kWh/m²) since heating demand is also
normalised by the same gross area.
